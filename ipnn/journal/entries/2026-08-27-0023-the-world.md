## In plain words

Javid designed a world out loud. Not a task, not an experiment — a **place**,
with a body in it: a grid that is bigger than the organism can see, an eye that
looks at part of it, a hand that can reach out and change it, and a mouth that
is not on the grid at all and exists only to say things. Plus a clock: the
world has a time of day, the organism can see what time it is, and the world's
rules can depend on it.

He flagged it himself as possibly too complex for a first build, asked for it
to be captured so it is not lost, and asked for a simpler starting version.
This entry is the capture. The simplification is the conversation that follows
it.

The reason it deserved its own entry rather than a line in a backlog file:
**three existing tracks each own one piece of this and none of them owns the
contract**, which is why the project keeps redefining its world per experiment
and cannot compare results across games.

## The idea, as stated [J]

> "Maybe it's just the eye and mouth design we already have but the mouth is
> either a bigger register, or there is a 'hand' and the hand can actually
> write to the frame that the eye sees. Maybe the grid is the world, the eye is
> a layer that perceives the world, and there is a hand and a mouth. The hand
> can touch the grid and interact with it (in different ways depending on the
> game). And the mouth is not on the grid but is maybe a smaller grid or array,
> where it can communicate different outputs."

> "1. The grid can be larger than what the eye can see, so the AI can
> effectively turn its head to move the eye around on the grid. So the grid is
> 64x64 or 128x128 and the eye can see 8x8 maybe.
> 2. We can introduce a 'time of day' element where the grid has different
> properties throughout the day, and maybe even has a row that shows its 'time
> of day' by enabling one box every 100 ticks. Then we can play time-based
> games where something is shown for 5 'seconds' (i.e. 5 boxes enabled over 100
> ticks), and then the AI has to say something or has to touch the displayed
> element.
> 3. Different properties of the world can take place at different times of day."

> "Maybe this is too complex of a world to start with. But the idea is that
> it's an ecosystem we define that allows us to create numerous scenarios /
> games, and then the entity just exists in this world and we continue to
> monitor how it interacts with the world and give it games / challenges, then
> we continue rewarding it as it gets those right."

## What is already registered, and what is new

**Already ours, and Javid re-derived it independently:**

| His element | Where it already lives |
|---|---|
| Grid bigger than the eye; turning the head | **§G rung 1** — "the world must be bigger than the sense… **this is the load-bearing change, and it is a change to the *world*, not the network**" |
| The hand touching and changing the grid | **§B1** — motor access to the grid, with the operant → contingency → external-memory ladder |
| Many games, one entity, continuous reward | **§H** + [H-017](../HYPOTHESES.md) |

That three tracks were re-derived from scratch in one paragraph is worth
noting: it is the second time in two days
([2026-08-26-2341](./2026-08-26-2341-adaptability-the-measure.md) §1 was the
first) that Javid has independently arrived at something the backlog already
holds. Read either as convergence on the real problem, or as a signal that the
backlog is too large to be navigable. Probably both.

**Genuinely new tonight, and both are structural rather than incremental:**

1. **The eye/hand/mouth split as a *body*, not as capabilities [J].** §B1
   framed motor access as a capability to add to an experiment. Javid framed it
   as **anatomy**: one channel that acts on the world, a different channel that
   communicates about it, and they are different organs. That is
   [H-018](../HYPOTHESES.md)'s perception-versus-communication factoring made
   **structural instead of hypothetical** — the separation stops being
   something we hope the substrate discovers and becomes something the body
   has. Registered as [H-030](../HYPOTHESES.md).
2. **Time of day [J].** The world carries a slow periodic phase, the organism
   can *see* it, and the world's rules can depend on it. Three distinct things
   in one mechanism — a perceivable slow variable ([H-003](../HYPOTHESES.md)'s
   problem attacked from the world's side rather than the organism's), ambient
   rule context (005's cue with the instruction removed), and the entire family
   of timing games. Registered as [H-029](../HYPOTHESES.md).

Both are filed into a new backlog track,
[§I — The World](../../experiment-ideas.md#i-the-world--one-body-many-rooms),
which exists because the *contract* had no owner.

## Analysis: what the clock is actually worth

The time-of-day idea deserves more than a backlog line, because it quietly
solves a problem the project has been stuck on from the wrong side.

[H-003](../HYPOTHESES.md) says slow learning requires a slow variable, and
[L-021–L-023](../LEARNINGS.md) established that 002 has none — 32,000 trials,
flat on every measure, persistence 5% throughout. Every attempt on that so far
has tried to give the *organism* a long time constant, and
[L-042](../LEARNINGS.md) shows how that goes: the one monotone quantity was
stored on the most ephemeral object and got taxed to death.

A world clock inverts it. The organism does not need to *hold* a long
timescale, only to **read** one. Long-horizon structure becomes a perceptual
problem rather than a memory problem — and this project's substrate is, on the
evidence, much better at perception than at memory.

Second-order consequence worth stating now so it is not claimed later as a
discovery: **the clock makes the world non-stationary on purpose**, which means
every game run in a clocked world needs its constant-clock control arm. A
result that only appears with the clock running is interesting; a result that
appears either way means the clock was decoration.

## Decisions

1. **New backlog track §I — The World**, holding the body/world contract. Its
   artefact is a *specification*, targeted by all later experiments, so that
   results become comparable across games instead of each experiment inventing
   its own world.
2. **H-029 and H-030 registered.**
3. **An entry gate is written into §I before any implementation exists**
   (deliberately, and while nobody is yet attached to a design): *the new world
   must reproduce a recorded result before it earns a second game.* If
   `identify` in the world does not match 001/003's recorded curves on the same
   seeds, the world is wrong and nothing built on it means anything.
4. **The infrastructure risk is named in the track itself.** L-012 caught this
   project building outward while the distinguishing mechanism stayed unbuilt;
   a world spec is precisely that shape of work, and saying so in the track is
   cheaper than rediscovering it.
5. **Nothing is promoted and the doctrine is unchanged.** An organism that
   cannot reliably learn one three-way task does not need a bigger world.

## Prior art & novelty

- **Similar:** the world-bigger-than-the-sensor and moving-fovea design is
  §G's, which is in turn Hawkins/Numenta's territory (reference frames),
  O'Regan & Noë's sensorimotor contingency, and Bajcsy's active perception. The
  hand acting on its own sensory surface is a standard closed sensorimotor
  loop. One-organism-many-games is Animal-AI and GVGAI. Interval timing is
  scalar timing theory (Gibbon 1977) and is measured across every vertebrate
  anyone has bothered to test.
- **Different:** the eye/hand/mouth separation as *anatomy* given up front, and
  a **perceivable world clock whose phase changes the rules** — context that is
  ambient rather than instructed.
- **Novel (claimed):** nothing yet, and the clock in particular smells like
  something the contextual-bandit and non-stationary-RL literatures have
  covered thoroughly under other names. *Unverified against literature* — this
  is now the third owed search from one session.

## Threats to validity

This entry records a design nobody has built or costed. The specific danger is
that a world spec is **satisfying to write and impossible to falsify** — it has
no gate of its own, which is why one was borrowed (decision 3) rather than left
to be invented later by someone who wants the world to succeed. The second
danger is scope: four organs, a clock, phase-dependent rules and a 64× larger
grid is a large build, and the honest reading of Javid's own "maybe this is too
complex to start with" is that he saw it first.
