## Lecture Notes

AI2 made ELMo in spring 2018, GPT (transformer-based ELMo) was released in summer 2018, BERT came out October 2018

Four major changes compared to ELMo:
- Transformers intstead of LSTMs
- Bidirectional model with "Masked LM" objective instead of normal LM
- Fine-tune instead of freeze at test time
- Operates over word pieces (byte-pair encoding)

First, let's explore Masked Language Modeling.

Elmo is a unidirectional model (as is GPT): we can concatenate two unidirectional models, but is this the right thing to do?

Example:
```
A stunning ballet dancer, Copeland is one of the best performers to see live.
```

Concatenating two unidirectional models might look like this:

```
"performer" <- |ELMo|
|ELMo| -> "ballet dancer"
```

The forward ELMo might tell us that Copeland is a ballet dancer, and the backward ELMo might tell us that Copeland is a performer.

These would be stapled / concatenated together, but they're not deeply merged.

Elmo representations look at each direction in isolation, while BERT looks at them joinly. 

```
        |BERT|
           |
           v
"ballet dancer / performer"
```

Reference: Devlin et al (2019)

### Bidirectional Modeling

How do we learn a "deeply bidirectionl" model? What happens if we just replace an LSTM with a transformer?

Transformers allow us to look at the context more broadly with self-attention.

It turns out that this doesn't work. Because, in language modeling you're trying to predict the next word, given the current word. So you have to be careful when setting up a model to ensure it doesn't look at the next word as you're feeding in data in batches. ELMo is set up so (even though it's just a neural network) it only looks at the past in order to predict the next word. And that's correct. But if we naively just threw transformers using self-attention, then the prediction of the next word could be informed from self-attention of the current word looking at the next word.

You could use a one-sided transformer (e.g. like GPT), but that doesn't solve the problem of looking from both directions in order to inform the representation of the current word.

### Masked Language Modeling

How do you prevent this "cheating"? Next word prediction fundamentally doesn't work for bidirectional models, instead we do ***masked language modeling***.

BERT formula: take a chunk of text, mask out 15% of the tokens, and try to predict them.

Input: 
```
[CLS] Text chunk 1 [SEP] Text chunk 2
```

50% of the time, you take the true next chunk of text, 50% of the time you take a random other chunk. Predict whether the next chunk is the "true" next.

Example (labeled as "NotNext"):
```
                 Madagascar                   enjoyed            like
[CLS]John visited [MASK] yesterday and really [MASK] it [SEP] / [MASK] Madonna.
```

Is the second chunk the true continuation of the first chunk or not? Why do this?
* Get more non-local reasoning
* Prediction gets localized at the CLS token

We have this CLS token that is useful for making a classification decision that looks at the entire input. The CLS token is what people use to aggregate information about the whole context. 

The overall BERT objective is to combine **masked LM** and **next sentence prediction**. 

Turns out in some future work that next-sentence prediction is not so important, but this gives us the intuition for how we can get the model to look at the context, look at the tokens in a bidirectional fashion, and how that's the foundation of how we build classifiers that are built on top of BERT.


## Further Notes / Research

### Practical use for an encoder-only model like BERT:

BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) is an encoder-only model. Instead of generating text, BERT is designed to understand the context of words in a sentence by looking at the words before and after it.

Uses:
- **Text Classification:** Given a piece of text, assign it to one or more predefined categories (e.g., sentiment analysis where texts are classified as positive, negative, or neutral).
- **Named Entity Recognition (NER):** Identify and classify entities in text into predefined categories like person names, organizations, locations, etc.
- **Question Answering:** Given a piece of text and a question related to it, identify the span of text that answers the question.
- **Text Similarity & Semantic Search:** Determine how similar two pieces of text are or search for documents semantically related to a query.
- **Text Embeddings:** BERT can generate dense vector representations of text that capture their semantic meaning.
- **Transfer Learning:** BERT can be fine-tuned on a small dataset to adapt to a wide variety of NLP tasks, leveraging its pre-trained knowledge.

### Pros/Cons of bidirectional models vs. causal models

**Bidirectional Models (e.g., BERT)**

Pros:
- Contextual Understanding: By looking at words both before and after the target word, these models often have a deeper understanding of context. This makes them very effective for tasks that require understanding the semantic meaning of text without generating new content.
- Fine-tuning: Can be easily fine-tuned on a variety of tasks with a small amount of task-specific data.

Cons:
- Not Generative: They're not designed to generate new sequences of text.
- Compute Intensity: Especially during training, these models can be computationally demanding due to their bidirectional nature.

**Causal Models (e.g., GPT)**

Pros:
- Generative Capability: Can generate new sequences of text, making them ideal for tasks like text generation, completion, and creative applications.
- Transfer Learning: Like BERT, they can also be fine-tuned on a wide variety of tasks.

Cons:
- Limited Context: For a given token, they only consider previous tokens and not future ones, which can sometimes limit the model's understanding in tasks that require full bidirectional context.
